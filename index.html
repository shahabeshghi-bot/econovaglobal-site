What the audit covers

  • Could you state your true inventory accuracy today? The audit measures it. → The KPI glossary
  • Suspect a problem but can’t size it? That is exactly what the Diagnostic tier is for.
  • About to spend on a redesign or a WMS? Baseline first, and the audit fee is credited toward implementation with us.
  • Inbound & receiving. First mile arrivals, dock scheduling, receiving checks and dock-to-stock time.
  • Put-away & slotting. Storage logic, slotting against velocity, travel distance and replenishment triggers.
  • Picking & packing. Pick methods, lines picked per hour, pack-bench flow and end-of-line packaging.
  • Outbound & dispatch. Order cut-offs, staging, dispatch accuracy, route planning and last mile handover.
  • Inventory accuracy & replenishment. Reorder point (ROP), safety stock, ordering rules and cycle counting, tested against actual demand, and your true inventory accuracy figure.
  • Labour & per-unit efficiency. Staffing against volume curves, productivity by process and cost per line shipped.
  • Layout & material flow. Travel paths, congestion points, storage media and space utilization.
  • Systems & WMS functionality. Whether the system supports the process, or the process is working around the system.
  • Distribution & channels. B2B distribution, foodservice distribution and the HoReCa channel, direct store delivery (DSD), 3PL performance, reverse logistics and cold chain integrity where temperature control matters.

Operational scope, line by line

For 3PL and fulfilment operations, the audit follows the work in this order: each step checked as it actually runs:

  • Goods-in & quality control. Counts, condition and paperwork checked at the dock before stock is accepted.
  • Put-away & location discipline. Every pallet and case to a recorded location, no floor stock by habit.
  • Lot, batch & expiry control. Traceability held by lot and batch, with date rotation where shelf life matters.
  • Quarantine & returns handling. Held stock kept physically separate; returns received, graded and decided without delay.
  • Pick & pack accuracy. Method, checks and bench flow that protect the order and the cost per order.
  • Value-added work. Labelling, repacking and mix casing set up as planned, costed work, not favours at the bench.
  • Cross-docking. Inbound flowed straight to outbound where a touch in storage adds no value.
  • Despatch & proof of delivery. Orders closed only when despatch is confirmed and proof of delivery is captured.

How it works

Five steps, agreed in a fixed scope before we start.

  1. Scope & data request

    We agree the fixed scope, covering facility, processes and channels, and request the operating data: order profiles, volumes, staffing, layout, system reports.

  2. Data analysis

    Your numbers first: throughput, order cycle time, inventory and demand patterns, labour against volume. The analysis frames what we test on site.

  3. On-site review

    We walk the full flow: receiving, put-away, slotting, picking, packing, dispatch, observing processes as they actually run, not as the SOP says they run.

  4. KPI baseline & findings

    Findings are quantified against the KPI baseline: OTIF, fill rate, dock-to-stock time, lines picked per hour, inventory accuracy, cost per line.

  5. The written report

    The engagement ends with the deliverable: strengths, weaknesses, and a prioritised roadmap, quick wins first, then the structural fixes with estimated impact.

Audit FAQ

What is a warehouse operations audit?

A fixed-scope, independent review of how your warehouse or distribution centre actually runs, receiving through dispatch, inventory through last mile, measured against the KPIs the industry runs on, and delivered as a complete written report covering strengths and weaknesses.

Why does it matter?

Warehouses drift. Volumes, SKU profiles and channels change, but slotting, replenishment parameters and staffing patterns stay where they were set. The audit finds where throughput, service level and cost have quietly diverged from what the operation was designed for, and prices the fixes in priority order before you spend on the wrong one.

What types of audit are there?

We begin with a free audit of your operation, no obligation, the entry step. After that, two paid tiers: a focused Diagnostic Audit, a health check of the core warehouse flow with headline KPIs, and a Full Operations Audit, which adds inventory and replenishment systems, distribution channels, last mile, reverse logistics and cold chain, with a full KPI baseline and a roadmap with estimated impact. See the comparison table above.

What does the audit examine?

Inbound receiving and dock-to-stock time; put-away and slotting; picking and packing; outbound dispatch and last mile handover; inventory accuracy, reorder point (ROP), safety stock and cycle counting; labour and per-unit efficiency; layout and material flow; systems and WMS functionality; and the end-of-line packaging operation.

What do we receive at the end?

A complete written report: your KPI baseline, strengths and weaknesses process by process, what each weakness is costing you, and the fixes in priority order, quick wins first, then structural changes with estimated impact. The report is yours to act on with your in-house team, or to implement with us, in which case the audit fee is credited toward the implementation.

End-of-Line Packaging Optimization

The pack bench is an operation in its own right. We engineer packing methods, materials and right-sizing for throughput, product protection and cost per order, including the value-added services (VAS) that share the bench: kitting, labelling and co-packing.

You need this when…

  • Packaging spend rises faster than order volume.
  • Damage and returns trace back to pack method or carton choice, feeding reverse logistics cost.
  • The pack bench is the bottleneck at peak, whatever the picking rate.
  • Cartons ship mostly air. Dimensional weight charges say so.

What we do

  • Packing process review: bench layout, methods, staffing and flow from pick to dispatch.
  • Materials & right-sizing: carton range, void fill, stretch and strap choices matched to the product profile, protection without overspend.
  • VAS design: kitting, labelling and co-packing set up as planned work with a cost per unit, not favours at the bench.
  • BC EPR in view: British Columbia’s extended producer responsibility (EPR) regime puts packaging obligations on the businesses that supply packaging into BC. We factor recyclability and reporting into material choices, so the cheapest option today is not a compliance problem tomorrow.

Where it sits

Packaging belongs to Layers 1 and 2 in our framework: it is designed, then implemented at the bench, and it is also a standard section of the operations audit, costed per order shipped.

Do you sell packaging equipment or materials?

No. Like our WMS work, packaging advice is independent: we specify methods and materials against your cost and protection targets, and you buy from your own suppliers.

What is right-sizing?

Matching the carton (or mailer) to the order instead of defaulting to two or three box sizes. It cuts void fill, damage from movement in the box, and dimensional-weight shipping charges, usually the fastest payback on the bench.

What does BC’s EPR regime mean for us?

Under extended producer responsibility, producers, the brands and importers who supply packaged goods into BC, carry obligations for the packaging they put into the market, including fees tied to material type and recyclability. Material choices made at the pack bench feed directly into those costs.

Can this be a small engagement?

Yes. A packaging-line review is a named micro-scope inside the audit family: bench, materials and cost per order, reported the same way.

Inventory Control & Replenishment Systems

Inventory control built as a system: replenishment parameters, reorder point (ROP) and safety stock set from real demand patterns, so availability holds without capital sitting in overstock.

  • Stock-outs and overstock at the same time? The classic symptom of replenishment parameters set by feel.
  • Could you state your inventory accuracy today? We measure it, the figure every other decision depends on. → What the KPIs mean

You need this when…

  • Stock-outs and overstock coexist. The fill rate suffers while working capital climbs.
  • Reorder points were set once, by feel, and never revisited as demand shifted.
  • Inventory accuracy is unknown, or known to be poor. Cycle counting is sporadic or absent.
  • Ordering depends on one person’s memory and judgement.

What we do

  • Replenishment model & ordering rules: who orders, when, and how much, codified, not personal.
  • ROP & safety stock calculation: set from demand variability and lead time, by SKU class, so fast movers and slow movers are treated differently on purpose.
  • Cycle counting programmes: a counting rhythm that lifts and holds inventory accuracy without wall-to-wall stocktakes.
  • Accuracy baseline: we measure true inventory accuracy as part of the work, the figure every other decision depends on.

Where it sits

Inventory findings are a standard part of the operations audit; this service is the fix, designed, implemented, then held by performance monitoring.

What is a reorder point, in plain terms?

The stock level at which you order more, set so the replenishment arrives before you run out: demand during lead time, plus safety stock for variability. The KPI glossary defines ROP, safety stock and the related terms.

Will this reduce our inventory, or increase it?

Usually both, in different places: overstock comes down where safety stock was padding guesswork, and availability rises where reorder points were too low. The net working-capital effect depends on your current parameters, which the baseline measurement establishes.

Do we need a WMS first?

No. Replenishment logic and cycle counting work on disciplined processes with basic system support. If a WMS comes later, the parameters and counting programme carry over.

How is inventory accuracy measured?

By cycle count results: counted locations that match the system record exactly, divided by locations counted. It is the honest version of “we think stock is about right.”

Library · Self-check

Do you need a warehouse audit?

Eight questions. Answer for your operation as it runs today, not as the procedure manual describes it. Three or more “yes” answers and an audit is likely to surface findings worth acting on.

1. Inventory accuracy is a guess. You couldn’t state your true inventory accuracy today, or the last cycle count disagreed with the system often enough to be embarrassing.

2. Stock-outs and overstock coexist. Fill rate complaints arrive while working capital in slow stock keeps climbing.

3. Overtime is structural. The operation only makes its cut-offs with routine overtime or temp labour, even at normal volumes.

4. Slotting hasn’t been revisited in a year or more. Fast movers aren’t where the pickers need them; travel distance is set by history, not velocity.

5. Nobody owns the KPIs. OTIF, fill rate, dock-to-stock time and lines picked per hour are not measured, or measured but never compared to a baseline.

6. The process lives in people’s heads. Reorder points, put-away logic and dispatch rules depend on specific individuals; a departure would take the system with them.

7. Growth or change is coming. A move, a new channel, e-commerce fulfilment, a WMS decision or a 3PL change within the next year, on top of an operation that already strains.

8. The last project’s gains are slipping. A WMS go-live, a layout change or a new process delivered improvement that has quietly eroded since.

Library · Reference

Warehouse KPI glossary

The vocabulary of the audit and the monitoring retainer, every term defined in plain language, with what it reveals about an operation.

OTIF: On Time In Full
The share of orders delivered on the promised date, complete, with nothing short or substituted. The single best summary of whether distribution is keeping the promise sales made. Measured per order and per channel, foodservice and HoReCa customers watch it closely.
Fill rate
The share of ordered units (or lines) shipped from stock on the first attempt. Low fill rate with high inventory is the classic symptom of broken replenishment: the stock exists, just not the right stock.
Dock-to-stock time
Elapsed time from a vehicle arriving at receiving to the goods being put away and available to promise. Long dock-to-stock hides inventory that is physically present but commercially invisible, and stalls first mile flow into the building.
Lines picked per hour
Picking productivity: order lines completed per labour hour. Only meaningful alongside pick accuracy and the order profile, a site picking full pallets and a site picking single e-commerce units should never be compared raw.
Order cycle time
Time from order release to dispatch. The internal half of the delivery promise; when it grows, cut-offs move earlier and last mile options shrink.
Inventory accuracy
Measured by cycle counting: locations where the physical count matches the system record exactly, divided by locations counted. Every replenishment, slotting and service decision inherits the quality of this number.
Reorder point (ROP)
The stock level that triggers a replenishment order: expected demand during the supplier lead time, plus safety stock. Set from data, reviewed as demand shifts, not set once by feel.
Safety stock
The buffer held against demand and lead-time variability. Too little and fill rate breaks; too much and capital sleeps on shelves. It belongs on SKUs in proportion to their variability, not spread evenly.
Cycle counting
Counting a planned slice of locations every day instead of an annual wall-to-wall stocktake. Done properly, it both measures inventory accuracy and continuously corrects it, and finds the process errors causing the drift.
Slotting
Deciding which SKU lives where, based on velocity, size and handling. Good slotting shortens travel and lifting; stale slotting is invisible overtime, paid every shift.
Put-away
Moving received goods from the dock to their storage location and recording it. Put-away discipline decides dock-to-stock time and whether picking can find what receiving accepted.
Cost per line shipped
Total warehouse operating cost divided by lines shipped, the per-unit efficiency figure that lets a 3PL price honestly and lets any operation compare periods, shifts and sites on one scale.

WMS readiness guide

A warehouse management system amplifies whatever it finds. This guide is what to settle before selection, so the system lands on sound processes, clean data and requirements you chose, not a vendor’s template.

1. Fix the process before you automate it

Map receiving, put-away, slotting, picking, packing and dispatch as they actually run. Where the current process is broken (workarounds, double handling, knowledge held by one person), correct the design first. Configuring a WMS around a broken process produces a faster broken process, with licence fees.

2. Get the data honest

A WMS runs on master data: SKU dimensions and weights, pack sizes, location addresses, barcode discipline. Audit the data before migration, and measure your inventory accuracy by cycle count. Go-live on inaccurate stock records is the most common self-inflicted wound in WMS projects.

3. Write requirements from your order profile

Your channels decide the requirements: full-pallet B2B, foodservice case picking, e-commerce single-unit fulfilment, VAS like kitting and labelling, cold chain checks, DSD dispatch. List the processes the system must support, in the volumes and shapes you actually ship, then weight vendor evaluations against that list.

4. Select independently

Every vendor demo is choreographed around the vendor’s strengths. Insist on scripted scenarios drawn from your own operation, your ugliest order, your peak day, your returns flow, and score every candidate against the same script. Selection done by an adviser with no software to sell is selection you can defend to your board.

5. Plan go-live as an operation, not an event

Training, UAT with real orders, a cut-over plan, and hypercare staffing for the weeks after. Then, once the system has bedded in, run a post-implementation audit: did the project deliver what the business case promised? It is the step almost everyone skips and the only one that proves the investment.

The mistakes that cost the most

  • Buying features for the operation you wish you had, instead of the volumes you ship.
  • Automating undocumented processes and discovering the exceptions at go-live.
  • Migrating inventory records that were never verified.
  • No baseline: without pre-project KPIs, “improvement” becomes an opinion.
  • Ending the project at go-live, no monitoring, so the gains drift away within a year.
